PROJECT: Working title "Slime / Dorf Village"

VISION
A persistent, procedurally generated civilization sandbox combining:

- Minecraft / Terraria: Destructible terrain, mining, construction,
  freeform creativity, and environmental exploration.
- StarCraft / Age of Empires: Villager orders, settlements, resource
  logistics, technological progression, and strategic command.
- RimWorld / Dwarf Fortress: Autonomous inhabitants, individuality,
  emergent stories, and interconnected simulation systems.
- Cult of the Lamb / BALL x PIT: Complementary settlement and
  adventure progression without mandatory micromanagement.
- Factorio: Blueprints, automation, logistics, and scaling production.
- Kenshi: Skills improving through actual experience.
- Majesty: Indirect control, autonomous behavior, and incentives.
- Aground / Spore: Progression from primitive survival toward
  advanced technology and eventual space exploration.
- Stellaris: Divergent civilizations, exploration, diplomacy,
  and potential interstellar expansion.
- Scan-n-Scram: Procedural planets, research, alien ecosystems,
  and exploration-oriented progression.
- Warhammer 40K Orks: The possibility of collective belief
  influencing physical reality as one technological pathway.

These are inspirations, NOT a checklist of features to implement.

CORE FANTASY
The player guides a small group of living creatures from primitive
shelters into a thriving civilization.

You begin with a handful of inhabitants sleeping in leaves and grass.
Over time they gather and discover new materials, develop professions, construct
settlements, establish industries, and potentially reach other planets.

The same individuals and settlements exist across this progression.

The player is primarily a godlike overseer rather than a single
permanent character, but may optionally possess individual inhabitants
to perform actions directly.

The player can focus on building, exploration, management, or any
combination of these.

The civilization should remain functional when the player chooses
not to personally engage with one of those systems.

DESIGN PILLARS

1. AUTONOMOUS, LIVING INHABITANTS

Every inhabitant is an individual with:
- Inherited natural attributes.
- Genetic variation and mutations.
- Personality, preferences, and inclinations.
- Learned skills and professional experience.
- Needs, relationships, and leisure activities.
- A personal history.
- Permanent mortality.

Genetics influence natural aptitude and potential.

Skills improve through actual activity, including autonomous
activities performed during downtime.

No manual allocation of attribute or skill points.

A genetically gifted miner starts with potential, not expertise.

Explore diminishing returns, bounded genetic advantages, and
other mechanisms preventing an inevitable optimal super-species.

Villagers should be interesting individuals, not disposable numbers.

Their knowledge may be transferred to others through teaching,
observation, apprenticeship, or documentation.

2. PLAYER CONTROL AT MULTIPLE SCALES

Strategic:
"Assign five villagers to gather lumber."

Operational:
"Maintain 200 lumber in storage."

Creative:
"Construct this bakery using my saved blueprint."

Personal:
Possess a villager to explore, mine, construct, or fight directly.

Possession may provide a modest divine-inspiration benefit,
but must not make manual control mandatory or overwhelmingly optimal.

Automated and manually controlled actions should operate on the
same underlying world simulation and mechanics.

Evaluate top-down, isometric, side-view, and optional first-person
perspectives.

Investigate Delver-style billboard sprites as a way of presenting
2D inhabitants in a 3D environment.

Do not assume full 3D voxels are necessary without examining
development cost and gameplay benefits.

3. FREEFORM CONSTRUCTION

Players design buildings rather than simply placing finished structures.

A bakery is a bakery because it satisfies functional requirements,
such as appropriate equipment, available workspace, and materials.

Buildings may have:
- Functional room requirements.
- Assigned purposes and workers.
- Construction materials.
- Equipment and furniture.
- Efficiency and environmental considerations.
- User-created blueprints.

Once designed, structures can be replicated by autonomous builders.

Explore whether a room-based recognition system, explicit designation,
or hybrid approach best supports creative freedom.

Avoid forcing optimal layouts that make creative construction pointless.

4. PROCEDURAL EXPLORATION AND MINING

A persistent world featuring:
- Surface biomes and environmental variation.
- Destructible and constructible terrain.
- Underground geology and resources.
- Procedural caves and discoveries.
- Wildlife, hostile creatures, and hazards.
- Ruins, mysteries, and potentially other civilizations.
- Increasingly challenging environments.

Villagers can independently explore, mine, gather, and retreat.

The player may personally take over expeditions.

Resources and discoveries enable new technological possibilities.

Avoid arbitrary difficulty scaling based exclusively on distance
or depth. Consider environmental and logistical challenges.

5. EMERGENT TECHNOLOGICAL ADVANCEMENT

This is a core distinguishing feature.

Do NOT implement a rigid Civilization-style technology tree as
the primary progression mechanism.

Technologies should emerge from combinations of:
- Discoveries.
- Available materials.
- Existing capabilities.
- Experimentation.
- Villager aptitudes.
- Cultural practices.
- Environmental pressures.
- Accumulated knowledge.
- Player priorities.

Technological eras should be recognizable but descriptive.

A civilization might reach space through conventional engineering,
biotechnology, mysticism, or collective-belief mechanisms.

Different technological pathways should produce different
strengths, weaknesses, infrastructure, and societies.

Explore collective belief as an unusual possible mechanism by
which technologies function, subject to clear rules and limitations.

Do not assume every species follows the same progression.

Technology should unlock fundamentally new capabilities, not
merely numerical bonuses.

6. OPTIONAL PLAYSTYLES

Players may primarily:
- Build and decorate.
- Manage inhabitants.
- Optimize production.
- Explore and adventure.
- Lead expeditions.
- Research and experiment.
- Observe the simulation.

Each approach must offer satisfying progression.

The village should not become helpless because the player spends
several hours exploring.

Exploration should not be impossible because the player dislikes
base management.

Engaging with multiple systems should provide flexibility and
interesting synergies, without making specialization invalid.

Avoid mandatory daily chores, energy systems, artificial timers,
streak mechanics, and manipulative engagement loops.

7. GROWTH THROUGH AUTOMATION

Early-game commands are specific:
"Bob, collect that iron."

Later-game commands are higher-level:
"Maintain the settlement's iron supply."

Eventually:
"Establish and maintain an interplanetary supply network."

Progress should make the civilization more independently capable.

The player should always retain the option to zoom down to the
individual level and intervene.

8. PERSISTENT CIVILIZATION HISTORY

Buildings persist.
Villagers age and die.
Skills and knowledge are transmitted.
Technologies alter civilization.
Settlements accumulate history.

Significant events may be recorded:
- First discoveries.
- Major constructions.
- Notable individuals.
- Disasters.
- Expeditions.
- Technological milestones.
- Cultural developments.

The original settlement should remain meaningful even after
substantial technological advancement.

9. SPACE EXPLORATION AS LONG-TERM EXPANSION

Eventually, advanced civilizations may:
- Construct spacecraft.
- Explore other planets.
- Discover alien ecosystems.
- Build extraterrestrial settlements.
- Establish trade routes.
- Encounter other civilizations.
- Visit other players' worlds.

Multiplayer should initially favor consensual cooperation and
visiting, not unrestricted invasion or destruction.

CRITICAL SCOPE CONSTRAINT:
The first planet must work as a complete, enjoyable game.

Do not design space exploration as a prerequisite for enjoying
the initial experience.

Treat interplanetary gameplay as a long-term expansion enabled
by the established simulation systems.

10. CORE COHESION

This must not feel like several disconnected games.

Construction, exploration, progression, automation, and inhabitants
should operate on shared systems.

Every major mechanic must support at least one of these:
- Developing the civilization.
- Discovering the world.
- Expressing player creativity.
- Creating memorable emergent stories.

If a mechanic doesn't meaningfully contribute, challenge it.

DESIGN PHILOSOPHY

Prefer systemic depth over feature quantity.

Prefer emergent behavior over scripted content.

Prefer player expression over prescribed solutions.

Prefer simulation that creates interesting decisions over
simulation that merely consumes CPU.

Prefer transparent, understandable rules over hidden complexity.

Prefer delegation and automation over mandatory micromanagement.

Preserve creativity even as civilization becomes automated.

Make the first planet worth spending hundreds of hours on
before expanding to the stars.

Do not assume that more realistic means more fun.

Do not confuse a large feature list with a compelling game.
